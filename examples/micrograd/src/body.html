The one loop behind all of it

3Blue1Brown showed you what a network is and how it learns. Karpathy now builds the missing part: the machine that works out the slopes.

  1. 1

    3Blue1Brown, Chapter 1 3B1B 1 · 15:17A neural network is just a function. Numbers go in, numbers come out, and about 13,000 knobs (weights and biases) decide what happens in between.

  2. 2

    3Blue1Brown, Chapter 2 3B1B 2 · 9:11Learning means turning those knobs so the cost (how wrong the network is) gets smaller. To know which way to turn each knob, you need its slope. Grant says the method that computes all those slopes is called backpropagation, and he leaves it for a later video.

  3. 3

    Karpathy, this lecture AK · 0:25Karpathy builds backpropagation from zero, in about 100 lines of Python. That small library is micrograd. At the end of the lecture he uses it to train a tiny network.

Play with it

What it means

A real network has thousands or millions of weights, not two. The loop is the same; it just runs on every weight at once:

  • Forward pass: push the input through the network's math and get a prediction.
  • Loss: compare the prediction with the right answer and squeeze "how wrong" into one number. That's the height of the ball.
  • Backward pass: for every weight, find the slope of the loss. If I nudge this weight up a tiny bit, does the loss go up or down, and by how much? The two slopes together are the orange arrow. This is what micrograd computes.
  • Update: move every weight a small step against its slope, which is downhill. Then repeat.

The step size is called the learning rate. Too small and learning is slow. Too big and the ball jumps over the valley and bounces around. Karpathy hits exactly this at AK · 2:06:30.

Why we need this

Any model you train later runs this exact loop. Say you train a small GPT of your own: the forward pass reads some text and guesses the next word. The loss says how wrong that guess was. The backward pass finds the slope for each of millions of weights. The update nudges them all. micrograd is the same loop, small enough that you can see every number.

That's also why the lecture starts with derivatives (8:08). A slope is a derivative, so before he can compute millions of them he makes sure you can feel what one of them means.

Check yourself

In the loop, what goes wrong if you skip the backward pass?
You would still know how wrong you are (the loss, the ball's height), but not which way to turn each weight. The update step has no arrow to follow.
Drop the ball near the top between the two valleys. Why can two starting points end in different valleys?
The slope only knows about the ground right under the ball. Gradient descent always walks to the nearest low point it can reach downhill, which may not be the lowest one overall. 3Blue1Brown says the same at 6:20 in Chapter 2.

▶ 0:25 – 8:08Chapter 2 · "micrograd overview"

What micrograd is

micrograd keeps a record of every + and × you do, so that afterwards it can work out how much each input affected the final answer.

Play with it

What he means

  • Autograd is short for "automatic gradient". You write normal math, and the library works out the slopes for you.
  • Backpropagation is how it does that: start at the final output and walk backwards through the expression, one small operation at a time.
  • When he says a.grad is 138, he means: if you nudge a up a tiny bit, g goes up about 138 times as much. It's the same slope as in 3Blue1Brown's Chapter 2, for one input.
  • "Neural networks are just mathematical expressions." The inputs are the data and the weights. The output is the prediction, or the loss. So a tool that finds slopes for any expression can also train a network.
  • micrograd works on single numbers (scalars). PyTorch packs thousands of numbers into grids called tensors so the computer can process them all at the same time. The math does not change, only the speed.

Why we need this

Everything after this lecture (makemore, GPT, any model you train) uses PyTorch's autograd. That is this same engine, built for speed. Once you have built micrograd, loss.backward() in PyTorch will never feel like magic.

▶ 8:08 – 14:12Chapter 3 · "derivative of a simple function with one input"

The derivative: how much does the output react?

Push the input a tiny bit, measure how much the output moves, then divide by the push. That ratio is the derivative, which is the slope.

Play with it

What he means

f(x) = 3x² − 4x + 5 is a made-up function. Nothing about it is special; it's just a curve to practise on. At x = 3: 3·9 − 12 + 5 = 20.

He does not find the derivative with the algebra rules from school, because nobody can do that for a network with thousands of terms. He measures it instead:

  • Nudge: x = 3 becomes 3.001 (h = 0.001).
  • Measure: f(3.001) = 20.014003, so the output rose by 0.014003.
  • Divide: 0.014003 ÷ 0.001 = 14.003. That's very close to the true slope 6·3 − 4 = 14.

The sign is the first thing to read. Positive: moving right goes uphill. Negative: moving right goes downhill. Zero: flat, the bottom of the valley.

Why not make h as small as possible? A computer keeps about 16 significant digits. When h gets very small, the change in f is lost in rounding and the answer turns into garbage. Press the "h = 0.000000000000001" button to see it happen.

Why we need this

This number is what learning runs on. 3Blue1Brown 3B1B 2 · 5:51: "if the slope is positive, shift left; if negative, shift right." The green "downhill" arrow in the picture is exactly that rule. Later, x will be a weight and f will be the loss.

The trick of nudging and measuring has a name: a numerical gradient. Karpathy keeps using it to double-check his answers. At 37:52 he calls it a "gradient check".

▶ 14:12 – 19:09Chapter 4 · "derivative of a function with multiple inputs"

Many inputs: wiggle one at a time

With three inputs you ask the same question three times: hold two still, nudge one, measure.

Play with it

What he means

Start values: a = 2, b = −3, c = 10, so d = 2·(−3) + 10 = 4. This is the cell Karpathy types at 14:12.

  • Nudge a. a gets multiplied by b = −3, so each extra bit of a takes away three times as much from d. By hand: 2.0001 × (−3) + 10 = 3.9997. That's 0.0003 less, and −0.0003 ÷ 0.0001 = −3.
  • Nudge b. b gets multiplied by a = 2. Slope 2.
  • Nudge c. c is added straight in, so d moves exactly as much as c does. Slope 1.

Two rules fall out of this, and Karpathy uses them on every node from 32:10 onwards:

  • For ×, each input's slope is the other input's value.
  • For +, each input's slope is 1.

The notebook prints slope -3.000000000010772. The 0.000000000010772 at the end is floating-point rounding. The true answer is exactly −3.

Why we need this

Put the slopes together, (−3, 2, 1), and you have the gradient of d. In the picture, the first two are the orange arrow on the floor: it points in the direction that raises d fastest. That's the arrow from 3Blue1Brown 3B1B 2 · 11:19, where a gradient of (3, 1) means "the first input matters three times as much". Here a matters three times as much as c.

In a network there's one slope like this for every weight. 3B1B shows that list as a long column of numbers 3B1B 2 · 11:00.

▶ 19:09 – 32:10Chapter 5 · "starting the core Value object of micrograd and its visualization"

The Value object: numbers that remember

Karpathy wraps each number in a small object that also remembers which numbers made it, and how. Those memories join up into a graph that he can walk backwards later.

Play with it

What he means

  • Plain numbers forget. After 2.0 * -3.0, Python only has -6.0. It has no idea where that came from. A Value keeps _prev (the inputs that made it) and _op (the operation, as a string such as '*').
  • a + b is a shortcut. Python turns it into a.__add__(b). By writing __add__ and __mul__, Karpathy teaches Python what + and × mean for his objects. This is called operator overloading. Names with double underscores are called "dunder" methods.
  • __repr__ only controls how the object prints. Without it you'd see something like <__main__.Value object at 0x7f…>. If your cell prints Value(data=2.0), yours works.
  • draw_dot draws the graph with Graphviz. The round + and * nodes are only for the drawing. They are not Value objects.
  • grad starts at 0.0. Later it will hold "if I nudge this, how much does L change?". Zero means "no effect known yet".
  • Leaves are values made directly, like a, b, c, f. In a real network, some leaves are weights (we want their gradients so we can change them) and some are data (fixed; we never change them).

Why we need this

You can't walk backwards without a path. Every + and × leaves a breadcrumb (_prev, _op). At 32:10 backpropagation follows those breadcrumbs from L back to every leaf, using the two rules from the last section (× gives "the other value", + gives 1) at each step.

PyTorch does exactly the same. Every tensor that comes out of an operation stores a grad_fn, which is its own breadcrumb.

The Python in this chapter

  • class Value:a blueprint. Value(2.0) builds one object from it.
  • __init__(self, data)runs automatically when you write Value(2.0). self is the new object being built.
  • self.data = datastores the number on the object, so a.data reads it back.
  • __repr__(self)returns the text that print and the notebook show.
  • f"Value(data={self.data})"an f-string: the part in { } is replaced by its value.
  • __add__(self, other)for a + b: self is a, other is b. It returns a new Value.
  • _children=()a default argument: if you don't pass it, it's an empty tuple.
  • set(_children)a set: no duplicates and no order. That's why d._prev may print in any order.
  • _prev, _opthe leading underscore is a habit that means "internal, for the library's own use".

▶ 32:10 – 51:10Chapter 6 · "manual backpropagation example #1: simple expression"

Backprop by hand: the chain rule

Start at L with gradient 1. At every node, multiply the gradient arriving from above by that node's own local slope. Repeat until you reach the leaves. That is the whole algorithm.

Play with it: you do the backprop

What he means

  • Base case: dL/dL = 1. Nudge L by h and L moves by h.
  • One step back: L = d × f is a × node, so each input's local slope is the other one's value: dL/dd = f = −2 and dL/df = d = 4. At 34:54 he proves it from the definition: ((d+h)f − df)/h = f.
  • The crux (37:52): c is two steps away from L. We know how L reacts to d (−2) and how d reacts to c (1, because d = e + c). The chain rule says: multiply. dL/dc = −2 × 1 = −2.
  • A + node just passes the gradient through to all its inputs, because its local slopes are all 1. Karpathy says it "routes" or "distributes" the gradient.
  • Last step: e = a × b. dL/da = (−2) × b = (−2)(−3) = 6, and dL/db = (−2) × a = −4.
  • Gradient check: he nudges each leaf by h inside lol() and measures. Same numbers. The function is only a "staging area", so the nudges don't touch his real variables.

His one-line summary at 50:50: backprop is "a recursive application of chain rule backwards through the computation graph".

Why we need this

Every node only needs to know its own small job (its local slope). It never needs to see the whole graph. That is why backprop scales to networks with billions of nodes: each piece is tiny and simple, and the chain rule glues them together.

Optional, if you want Grant's picture of the same idea: 3B1B · Ch. 3 backprop and 3B1B · Ch. 4 the calculus. They aren't in your plan, and Karpathy covers everything you need.

▶ 51:10 – 52:52Chapter 7 · "preview of a single optimization step"

One optimization step

Move every leaf a little in the direction of its own gradient and L goes up. Move against it and L goes down. That small move is one step of learning.

Play with it

What he means

  • The gradient says which way to push each leaf to make L bigger. So a.data += 0.01 * a.grad pushes every leaf "uphill" by a small step.
  • He then reruns the forward pass by hand and L is less negative: −7.29 instead of −8.
  • Here he wants L to go up, just to show the power of the gradient. When training a network, L is the loss and we want it to go down, so we step against the gradient: that's the minus sign at 2:02:58.

This is box 4 ("Update") of the training loop at the top of the page. The 3D landscape there does the same thing with two weights instead of four leaves.

▶ 52:52 – 1:09:02Chapter 8 · "manual backpropagation example #2: a neuron"

Backprop through a neuron

A neuron multiplies each input by a weight, adds them up with a bias, and squashes the total with tanh. Backprop through it uses the same × and + rules, plus one new local slope: tanh's is 1 − tanh².

Play with it

What he means

  • The model of a neuron (53:28): inputs x, one weight w per input (the "synapse strength"), multiply them, add them all up, add the bias b (how "trigger happy" the neuron is), then pass the total through an activation function.
  • tanh squashes any number into the range −1 to 1. It's the same job as 3Blue1Brown's sigmoid, which squashes into 0 to 1.
  • He sets b = 6.8813735870195432 on purpose so the numbers come out nice: n = 0.8814 and o = 0.7071.
  • Backprop: o.grad = 1. tanh's local slope is 1 − o² = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5, so n.grad = 0.5. The two + nodes copy 0.5 to b and to both products. The × nodes give w1.grad = x₁ × 0.5 = 1, x1.grad = w₁ × 0.5 = −1.5, w2.grad = x₂ × 0.5 = 0, x2.grad = w₂ × 0.5 = 0.5.
  • w2.grad is 0 because x₂ is 0. If an input is zero, its weight can't change anything right now, so it gets no gradient.
  • Any function can be a node (58:10), as long as you know its local derivative. tanh isn't built from + and ×; it's one block with its own rule.

Why we need this

▶ 1:09:02 – 1:17:32Chapter 9 · "implementing the backward function for each operation"

A _backward for every operation

Instead of filling in gradients by hand, every operation leaves behind a tiny function that knows its own local rule. Call those functions in the right order and backprop runs itself.

Play with it

What he means

  • Inside __add__ he defines a small function _backward and stores it on out. It still remembers self, other and out after __add__ has returned. A function that remembers the variables around it like this is called a closure.
  • +: self.grad = 1.0 * out.grad and the same for other.
  • ×: self.grad = other.data * out.grad, other.grad = self.data * out.grad.
  • tanh: self.grad = (1 - t**2) * out.grad, where t is the output.
  • A leaf's _backward does nothing: lambda: None.
  • Before starting, o.grad must be set to 1.0. Otherwise everything gets multiplied by the starting 0.
  • The funny bug at 1:14:57: he wrote out._backward = _backward(). The brackets call the function right away and store its result (None). Without brackets you store the function itself to call later.

(Later, at 1:25:25, every = here becomes +=. The picture already uses +=. You'll see why in the bug section.)

Every operation in PyTorch has exactly this pair: a forward that computes the value and a backward that applies the local rule. At 2:23:30 Karpathy shows that adding your own operation to PyTorch means writing the same two functions.

The Python in this chapter

  • def _backward(): ... inside __add__a function defined inside another function. It can use self, other and out.
  • out._backward = _backwardstore the function on the object, without calling it.
  • self._backward = lambda: Nonea one-line function that does nothing: the default for leaves.
  • o._backward()now call it.

▶ 1:17:32 – 1:22:28Chapter 10 · "implementing the backward function for a whole expression graph"

The right order: topological sort

A node may only pass its gradient back once it has received everything from the nodes after it. A topological sort lists the nodes so that is guaranteed; walking the list backwards does the whole backprop.

Play with it

What he means

  • We must never call a node's _backward before every node that uses it has finished. Otherwise its gradient isn't complete yet.
  • Topological sort lays out a graph so all the arrows point one way (left to right). It works for a DAG: a directed acyclic graph, where arrows have a direction and never loop back.
  • build_topo(v): if v isn't visited, mark it, call build_topo on each child, and only then append v. So a node always comes after all of its children.
  • The list ends with o. Reversed, it starts with o, so gradients flow from the output back to the leaves.
  • Finally he hides all this inside backward() (no underscore), so you just write o.backward(), exactly like loss.backward() in PyTorch.

The Python in this chapter

  • visited = set()a set: checking "is it in there?" is fast.
  • def build_topo(v): ... build_topo(child)recursion: a function that calls itself on the smaller pieces.
  • topo.append(v)add to the end of the list.
  • for node in reversed(topo):walk the list from the end to the start.

▶ 1:22:28 – 1:27:05Chapter 11 · "fixing a backprop bug when one node is used multiple times"

The bug when a value is used twice

If a value feeds into two places, its gradient must be the sum of what both places send back. Writing = overwrites the first one; writing += adds them.

Play with it

What he means

  • Simplest case: b = a + a. The answer should be 2 (nudge a by h, b grows by 2h). But self and other are the same object, so the second other.grad = 1.0 * out.grad just sets it to 1 again.
  • The general rule (the multivariable chain rule): when a value reaches the output along several paths, add up the gradient from every path.
  • The fix is one character per line: += instead of =. It works because every grad starts at 0.

In a real network almost everything is used more than once: every input feeds many neurons, and every weight is used for every example. Accumulating with += is what makes that work. The flip side shows up at 2:10:38: because gradients accumulate, you must reset them to zero before each new backward pass.

accumulateيتراكم (يجمع)add each new amount onto what's already there
overwriteيكتب فوقه (يستبدل)replace the old value, losing it
multivariable chain ruleقاعدة السلسلة لعدة متغيراتadd the contributions from every path

▶ 1:27:05 – 1:39:31Chapter 12 · "breaking up a tanh, exercising with more operations"

Building tanh from smaller pieces

tanh can be built from exp, +, − and ÷. Built either way, the output and every gradient come out exactly the same. How big your building blocks are is your choice.

Play with it

What he means

  • Mixing in plain numbers (1:28:15): a + 1 fails because 1 has no .data. Fix: if other isn't a Value, wrap it: Value(other).
  • __rmul__ (1:29:26): the "reflected" multiply. Python calls it when the left side doesn't know how to multiply.
  • exp: the derivative of ex is ex itself, so self.grad += out.data * out.grad.
  • Division is rewritten as a × b−1, so he only needs a power operation. Its local slope is the power rule: k·xk−1.
  • Subtraction is a + (−b), and −b is b × −1.
  • Then tanh = (e2x − 1)/(e2x + 1), and the gradients match the single tanh node.

Any operation, small or big, is fine as long as it has a forward and a local backward. PyTorch has both tiny operations and big fused ones for speed. You will meet this again when Karpathy writes a whole layer's backward pass by hand in lecture 5 ("backprop ninja").

Training: gradient descent for real

Forward, loss, zero the grads, backward, update, repeat. Below, a real micrograd (rewritten in JavaScript, rule for rule) trains Karpathy's network on his four examples while you watch.

Play with it

What he means

  • The update: p.data += -0.01 * p.grad for all 41 parameters. The minus sign (2:02:58): the gradient points uphill (toward a bigger loss), and we want to go down.
  • Check: one weight was 0.854 with a negative gradient. After the step it's 0.857: it went up, which lowers the loss.
  • Repeat forward → backward → update and the loss falls: 4.84, 4.36, 3.9, 3.66, …
  • Learning rate: too small is slow; too big can overshoot and "blow up". 0.01 was too small and 0.1 "dangerously" high, so he uses 0.05.
  • The bug (2:10:38): he forgot to reset the grads to 0 before backward(). Because backward uses +=, every step's gradients piled on top of the old ones. It only "worked" because the problem is so easy.
  • The fix: for p in n.parameters(): p.grad = 0.0 before each backward. In PyTorch that's zero_grad().

This is the exact loop that trains a GPT of your own later. The pieces get upgraded: Adam instead of this plain update, cross-entropy instead of squared error, random batches of examples instead of all of them at once. But it is still forward, loss, zero_grad, backward, update, repeat. And this is where the 3D landscape at the top of the page comes alive: the network is a ball rolling down a 41-dimensional hill.

Before you call it understood

A good bar: you can explain it out loud without notes. Answer these five out loud, then open them to compare.

1 · What does a.grad = 6 tell you, in one sentence?
If you nudge a up by a tiny amount, the output goes up by about 6 times that amount.
2 · Why do gradients flow backwards and not forwards?
We want the output's sensitivity to every node. The output's gradient on itself is 1, and each node's gradient can be computed from its parent's (chain rule). So we start at the output and work back to the leaves.
3 · What are the local rules for + and ×?
+ passes the gradient through unchanged (local slope 1). × gives each input the other input's value times the upstream gradient.
4 · Why += in _backward, and why zero_grad before each backward?
+= adds up contributions when a value is used in several places. Because of that, old gradients from the previous step would pile up too, so they must be reset to 0 first.
5 · Describe one training step of the MLP from start to finish.
Forward the four inputs to get predictions; compute the loss (sum of squared errors); zero every grad; loss.backward(); move every parameter a small step against its gradient (p.data -= lr * p.grad).
